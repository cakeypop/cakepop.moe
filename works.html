Kraken Submersibles - Nautilus AUV

Kraken Submersibles is New Mexico State University's first entry into RoboSub, an international autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) competition. I served as Embedded Systems & Autonomy Lead on a 7-person team, starting from zero in September 2025 - no prior team history, no established budget, and no existing architecture to inherit. The team's website (which I built, linked below) scored 18th of 58 international teams on that portion of judging, and the vehicle qualified at competition.

Systems Architecture

I authored the complete power and data architecture for the vehicle - every subsystem interface, from dual 14.8V LiPo batteries through the PM07 power distribution board to eight thrusters, and from four hydrophones through a custom preamp/ADC into the Jetson Orin Nano's I2S bus. This was the single largest time investment of the project: verifying, validating, and illustrating the system down to individual wires and how they communicate, then testing each component piecewise before final integration.

Hydrophone Preamp PCB

I designed a 4-channel JFET hydrophone preamplifier and ADC board from scratch, adapting a reference topology (Alex Rice / Zach Poff) into a full production design in KiCad. The board takes signal from four Aquarian AS-1 hydrophones, preamplifies it through discrete JFET stages, and digitizes all four channels synchronously through a PCM1865 ADC over I2S to the Jetson.

The design went through several major iterations over months of work: an initial hand sketch, a breadboard prototype to validate the analog stage, and recurring design reviews with Dr. Furth, a former EE professor, to pressure-test the circuit before committing to a board. The final version splits the design into mother and daughter boards to isolate analog and digital signal domains - a change made specifically to keep the noise floor clean and make future troubleshooting easier.

  • Reverse-polarity protection, status LEDs for power/COMM, resettable fuse, and 0-ohm test-point resistors for troubleshooting
  • Fabricated through JLCPCB (5-board minimum run), including their DFM verification process
  • Hand-soldered all non-SMD components and bring-up tested before competition
  • Validated the I2S data path end-to-end using a UMC404HD as a test-bench substitute for the Jetson

Board photos and KiCad renders coming soon - full TDOA (time-difference-of-arrival) testing is planned once the sub is back in the lab.

Program & Budget Ownership

Before the team had confirmed funding, I built a tiered technical budget so we could make component decisions at whatever funding level we ended up with, rather than stalling on procurement. I owned every line of the bill of materials except frame/mechanical material selection.

  • $7,500: minimum viable build - OEM parts kept to a minimum, maximizing in-house fabrication
  • $11,000: entry-level DVL added, with tradeoffs elsewhere to compensate
  • $15,000: Nucleus-tier DVL, rebalancing quality across the rest of the BOM
  • $20,000+: full upgrade path - freedom to improve every subsystem

I also led the team's sponsorship outreach to Nortek Group, attending every meeting through to close - the result was a Nortek Nucleus1000 DVL at a $5,000 discount, with NMSU covering the remaining $5,000. Separately, I organized and initially fronted $4,250 in travel costs (hotel and rental car for a 7-person team) and secured reimbursement funding from NMSU through direct outreach to Dr. Garcia.

Competition Execution

At competition, working autonomy wasn't ready. Rather than scratch the run, I wrote a script to hard-code a command sequence that let the vehicle execute a qualifying run without full autonomy - on an average of two hours of sleep a night across the event. In the days before travel, I got help from a friend outside the team (Wen), and we worked tirelessly building and testing computer-vision scripts at the NMSU pool, trying to get autonomy working in time. It didn't make the deadline, but the qualifying run held.

I've also kept detailed project logs since September 2025 and organized the team's datasheet archive, and I'm working on a continuity log to help next year's capstone team pick up the project from where we finished.

Rubik's Cube Simulation Project

Through the Electrical & Computer Engineering curriculum at NMSU, there is an Embedded Systems course (EE 112) which features a final project in Arduino C++ which allows freedom of design to the student as long as it implements the topics learned throughout the course. For my project, I created a simulation of a Rubik’s Cube.

I wrote a program that utilizes an LCD Shield integrated with an Arduino Uno to display a Rubik’s cube graphically in real time as it is being manipulated by moves entered through the Arduino GUI. The Rubik’s cube is shown both in a dimetric perspective, as well as in an unfolded 2D representation on the LCD screen. There is also ASCII imagery representing the unfolded 2D map as output to the Serial monitor. The simulation of the Rubik’s cube was achieved through the use of arrays corresponding to each of the fifty-four spots which can be manipulated, but must always remain consistent with the behavior of a real-life model. (e.g. the center square of each face never changes, and there are always nine of each of the six colors) The moveset, or possible turns of a cube, can be simplified to six types of turns, where every other type of manipulation can be defined as a combination of those six turns. Those six turns are:

  • Front - The closest third of the cube is turned ClockWise (CW)
  • Right - The right third of the cube is turned CW
  • Up - The top third of the cube is turned CW
  • Left - The left third of the cube is turned CW
  • Back - The back third of the cube is turned CW
  • Down - The bottom third of the cube is turned CW
Through the use of OSS and Thin-Film Transistor(TFT) libraries available online, I utilized my own 240x320 pixel-art of what I envisioned the graphical display should look like to map out a series of programmed functions that draw the lines and fill in the shapes resembling a cube. Then I called those functions to update the graphical display as the virtual cube is manipulated. Rigorously edge-testing and debugging until I couldn’t make it fail. There is an extensive log of the process here. (PDF hosted on Google Drive)

The above video is the demo showcasing my project that simulates a Rubik's Cube opn an Arduino Uno with a TFT LCD Shield. In the video, I send a series of inputs(turns) through the Arduino GUI, that manipulate the cube from a solved state to the pattern matching the physical cube on the webcam. The pattern gives the illusion of a cube inside of a cube inside of a cube, and I used it because it's my favorite pattern that can be made on a Rubik's cube. As the inputs are sent to the Arduino Uno, the LCD screen updates both the unfolded 2D and 3D dimetric map of the virtual cube.

3D Printing

Several years ago, I took a course which allowed me to become proficient in SolidWorks CAD as well as gain experience with drafting 2D blueprints alongside 3D models. As a result, I eventually acquired my own 3D printer, an Ender 3 v2. Since getting it in 2021, I have spent much of my free time creating models in SolidWorks, SketchUp, and Autodesk CAD, and utilizing the combination of them as a resource for both problem solving and entertainment purposes.
Much of my time was spent learning to work with OSS such as Cura Slicer to dial in a printer with both my own personal models as well as models posted online to sites like Thingiverse.
